How CTEC English A works

CTEC allows learners to earn recognition for successful completion of individual modules. English A is organised around three ways of communicating: informative, literary and persuasive discourse. Completing modules can contribute towards wider qualifications under CXC rules.

This is a Babonneau Secondary School revision guide, not an official CXC syllabus or examination paper. Check the current syllabus and your teacher's instructions for formal assessment requirements.

Module 1: Informative Discourse

Learning goal: Identify, organise and communicate information accurately and clearly.

Mini lesson: Facts, opinions and supporting details

A fact can be checked against evidence. An opinion expresses a judgement or belief. A strong informative paragraph presents a central point and develops it with relevant details.

Example: "Our school library opens at eight o'clock" is checkable. "The library is the best place in school" expresses a judgement.

Try it yourself

Read a short school announcement. Identify its audience, purpose and three key facts. Write a 60–80 word summary without including your personal opinion.

Module 2: Literary Discourse

Learning goal: Interpret literary writing using textual evidence and communicate ideas creatively.

Mini lesson: Character and conflict

Character traits are shown by actions, dialogue, thoughts and decisions. Conflict is the struggle that drives events in a story. When explaining an interpretation, name an example from the text and explain what it shows.

Try it yourself

Write a 150-word story in which a student must make a difficult choice. Make the conflict clear and show at least one character trait through action rather than direct description.

Module 3: Persuasive Discourse

Learning goal: Evaluate arguments and communicate a supported point of view.

Mini lesson: Claim, reason, evidence and response

A persuasive paragraph begins with a clear claim, adds a relevant reason, supports it with evidence or an example and explains why the evidence matters. A thoughtful argument may acknowledge a different viewpoint and respond respectfully.

Try it yourself

Write a 180–220 word letter arguing whether schools should allocate more time to reading. Include a clear position, two reasons, one counterargument and a response.

Suggested weekly study routine

Day 1: Read the module overview and one sample text. Day 2: Practise comprehension or analysis. Day 3: Complete one writing task. Day 4: Revise grammar, vocabulary and sentence control. Day 5: Review corrections and ask your teacher for feedback.
